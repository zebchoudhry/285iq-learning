topic: Bonding

lesson: 2.1 - Formation of Ions
MC | 1 | What is an ion? | An atom or group of atoms with a charge | An atom with extra neutrons in it | A molecule made of two atoms | An atom with an empty outer shell | Ions form when atoms lose or gain electrons, so they carry a charge.
MC | 2 | What happens when a sodium atom forms an ion? | It loses one electron | It gains one electron | It loses one proton | It gains one neutron | Sodium (2,8,1) loses its outer electron to form Na⁺ with a full outer shell.
MC | 2 | What is the charge on a magnesium ion? | 2+ | 1+ | 2- | 1- | Magnesium is in Group 2, so it loses two electrons to form Mg²⁺.
MC | 2 | What is the charge on an oxide ion? | 2- | 2+ | 1- | 6- | Oxygen is in Group 6, so it gains two electrons to form O²⁻.
MC | 3 | Why does a chlorine atom gain an electron rather than lose seven? | Gaining one is far easier than losing seven | Losing electrons would make it a metal | Its nucleus pulls in only one electron | It has no electrons to lose | Gaining one electron gives a full outer shell with much less energy than losing seven.
MC | 3 | What is the electronic structure of a sodium ion, Na⁺? | 2,8 | 2,8,1 | 2,8,2 | 2,7 | Na (2,8,1) loses its outer electron, leaving 2,8, the same as neon.
SA | 2 | What sign of charge do ions formed from metals have? | positive | Metals lose electrons, so their ions are positive.
SA | 2 | How many electrons does an aluminium atom lose to form its ion? | 3 | Aluminium is in Group 3, so it loses 3 electrons to form Al³⁺.

lesson: 2.2 - Ionic Bonding
MC | 1 | Between which types of element does ionic bonding happen? | A metal and a non-metal | Two non-metals | Two metals | A noble gas and a metal | Metals transfer electrons to non-metals, forming oppositely charged ions.
MC | 2 | What holds the ions together in an ionic compound? | Strong attraction between opposite charges | Shared pairs of electrons between atoms | A sea of delocalised electrons | Weak forces between small molecules | Ionic bonds are strong electrostatic forces between positive and negative ions.
MC | 2 | In the formation of sodium chloride, what happens to the electrons? | One moves from sodium to chlorine | One moves from chlorine to sodium | Sodium and chlorine share a pair | Both atoms lose an electron | Sodium transfers its outer electron to chlorine, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
MC | 2 | What does a dot and cross diagram show? | Where the electrons in each atom came from | The 3D shape of the giant lattice | How many neutrons each atom has | The size of each of the ions | Dots and crosses show which atom each electron came from.
MC | 3 | Magnesium reacts with oxygen. How many electrons move from each magnesium atom? | 2 | 1 | 3 | 6 | Mg loses 2 electrons and O gains 2, forming Mg²⁺ and O²⁻.
MC | 3 | What is one limitation of a dot and cross diagram? | It does not show the 3D arrangement of ions | It does not show which ion is positive | It does not show the electrons at all | It shows the wrong number of atoms | Dot and cross diagrams show electron transfer, not the shape of the lattice.
SA | 2 | What is the formula of sodium chloride? | NaCl | One Na⁺ balances one Cl⁻.
SA | 2 | What type of bonding is found in magnesium oxide? | ionic | Magnesium is a metal and oxygen a non-metal, so the bonding is ionic.

lesson: 2.3 - Ionic Compounds
MC | 1 | What is the structure of an ionic compound? | A giant lattice of ions | Small separate molecules | Long chains of atoms | Layers with free electrons | Ions form a regular giant lattice held by strong forces in all directions.
MC | 2 | Why do ionic compounds have high melting points? | Many strong forces between ions must be broken | The molecules inside them are very large | There are weak forces between molecules | They contain free electrons that hold them | Lots of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic forces throughout the lattice.
MC | 2 | When do ionic compounds conduct electricity? | When melted or dissolved in water | Only when they are solid | Never, under any conditions | Only when cooled below freezing | When melted or dissolved, the ions are free to move and carry charge.
MC | 3 | Why does solid sodium chloride not conduct electricity? | Its ions are fixed in place and cannot move | It has no ions in it when it is solid | Its electrons are all shared in bonds | Its molecules have no overall charge | Charge needs to move. In a solid, the ions are held in fixed positions.
MC | 2 | What is the formula of magnesium chloride? | MgCl2 | MgCl | Mg2Cl | Mg2Cl3 | Mg²⁺ needs two Cl⁻ ions to balance its charge.
MC | 3 | What is the formula of aluminium oxide? | Al2O3 | AlO | Al3O2 | AlO3 | 2 × Al³⁺ (+6) balance 3 × O²⁻ (-6).
SA | 2 | What is the formula of calcium oxide? | CaO | Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ balance one to one.
SA | 3 | What is the formula of sodium oxide? | Na2O | Two Na⁺ ions balance one O²⁻ ion.

lesson: 2.4 - Molecular & Empirical Formulas
MC | 1 | What does a molecular formula show? | The actual number of each type of atom in a molecule | The simplest whole number ratio of atoms | The charges on the ions in a compound | The shape of the molecule in 3D | A molecular formula gives the true number of atoms, for example C2H6 for ethane.
MC | 2 | What does an empirical formula show? | The simplest whole number ratio of atoms | The actual number of each atom in a molecule | The mass of one mole of the compound | The bonds between the atoms | The empirical formula is the simplest ratio, for example CH3 for ethane.
MC | 2 | What is the empirical formula of C2H4? | CH2 | C2H4 | CH4 | C4H8 | Divide by 2: C2H4 → CH2.
MC | 2 | What is the empirical formula of C6H12O6? | CH2O | C6H12O6 | C2H4O2 | CHO | Divide by 6: C6H12O6 → CH2O.
MC | 3 | A compound has the empirical formula CH2 and a relative formula mass of 42. What is its molecular formula? | C3H6 | C2H4 | CH2 | C4H8 | CH2 has Mr 14. 42 ÷ 14 = 3, so the formula is C3H6.
MC | 3 | Which compound has the same molecular and empirical formula? | CH4 | C2H6 | C2H4 | H2O2 | CH4 cannot be simplified further. The others can be divided down.
SA | 2 | What is the empirical formula of C2H6? | CH3 | Divide by 2: C2H6 → CH3.
SA | 3 | What is the empirical formula of hydrogen peroxide, H2O2? | HO | Divide by 2: H2O2 → HO.

lesson: 2.5 - Covalent Bonding
MC | 1 | What is a covalent bond? | A shared pair of electrons | A transfer of electrons | An attraction between ions | A sea of free electrons | In a covalent bond two atoms share a pair of electrons.
MC | 1 | Between which types of element do covalent bonds usually form? | Non-metals | Metals | A metal and a non-metal | Noble gases | Non-metal atoms share electrons to get full outer shells.
MC | 2 | How many covalent bonds does a carbon atom form in methane, CH4? | 4 | 2 | 1 | 3 | Carbon has 4 outer electrons, so it forms 4 bonds to get a full shell.
MC | 2 | What type of bond holds the two atoms together in an oxygen molecule, O2? | A double covalent bond | A single covalent bond | An ionic bond | A metallic bond | Each oxygen needs two more electrons, so they share two pairs: a double bond.
MC | 3 | How many shared pairs of electrons are in a molecule of nitrogen, N2? | 3 | 1 | 2 | 4 | Nitrogen needs three more electrons, so the two atoms share three pairs: a triple bond.
MC | 2 | How many covalent bonds does each hydrogen atom form? | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | Hydrogen has one electron and needs one more, so it forms one bond.
SA | 2 | How many covalent bonds are in a molecule of water, H2O? | 2 | Oxygen forms one bond with each of the two hydrogen atoms.
SA | 2 | How many electrons are in a single covalent bond? | 2 | A single covalent bond is one shared pair: two electrons.

lesson: 2.6 - Types of Covalent Structures
MC | 1 | Which substance is made of small molecules? | Water | Diamond | Sodium chloride | Copper | Water is a simple molecular substance. Diamond is giant covalent.
MC | 2 | Why do small molecules have low boiling points? | The forces between the molecules are weak | The covalent bonds inside are weak | They are held in a giant lattice | They contain free moving electrons | Boiling only breaks the weak intermolecular forces, not the covalent bonds.
MC | 2 | Why do small molecules not conduct electricity? | They have no overall charge and no free electrons | Their molecules are held in a lattice | Their covalent bonds are too strong | They are always gases at room temperature | Molecules are neutral and have no delocalised electrons to carry charge.
MC | 3 | As small molecules get larger, how do their boiling points change? | They increase | They decrease | They stay the same | They drop to absolute zero | Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed.
MC | 2 | Which of these is a giant covalent structure? | Silicon dioxide | Carbon dioxide | Methane | Ammonia | Silicon dioxide (silica) has a huge network of covalent bonds.
MC | 3 | Why do giant covalent structures have very high melting points? | Many strong covalent bonds must be broken | Weak forces between the molecules break | Ions must be pulled away from each other | Delocalised electrons must be removed | Melting means breaking lots of strong covalent bonds throughout the structure.
SA | 2 | What type of structure do polymers such as poly(ethene) have? | molecular | Polymers are very large molecules held by covalent bonds, with forces between the chains.
SA | 3 | Boiling a simple molecular substance overcomes the weak forces between molecules. What one word, starting with 'inter', names these forces? | intermolecular | Only the intermolecular forces are overcome. The covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact.

lesson: 2.7 - Diamond & Graphite
MC | 1 | Which element are diamond and graphite both made of? | Carbon | Silicon | Oxygen | Nitrogen | Diamond and graphite are both forms of pure carbon.
MC | 2 | How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in diamond? | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | Every carbon bonds to four others, making a rigid giant structure.
MC | 2 | Why is diamond very hard? | Each atom has four strong covalent bonds | Its layers slide over each other | It has free moving delocalised electrons | It is held together by ionic bonds | The rigid network of strong bonds makes diamond very hard.
MC | 2 | Why is graphite soft and slippery? | Its layers can slide over each other | Its atoms are joined by weak bonds | It is made of small separate molecules | It has no covalent bonds at all | There are no covalent bonds between the layers, so they slide easily.
MC | 3 | Why does graphite conduct electricity but diamond does not? | Graphite has one delocalised electron per carbon | Diamond has ions that cannot move | Graphite is a metal and diamond is not | Diamond has no electrons at all | In graphite each carbon bonds to three others, leaving one electron free to move.
MC | 2 | How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in graphite? | 3 | 4 | 2 | 1 | Each carbon bonds to three others, forming hexagonal layers.
SA | 1 | Which form of carbon is used in pencils? | graphite | Graphite's layers slide off onto the paper.
SA | 2 | What shape are the rings of carbon atoms in graphite? | hexagons | Graphite's layers are made of hexagonal rings.

lesson: 2.8 - Graphene & Fullerenes
MC | 1 | What is graphene? | A single layer of graphite | A ball-shaped carbon molecule | A form of diamond | A carbon polymer chain | Graphene is one layer of graphite, one atom thick.
MC | 2 | Which property makes graphene useful in electronics? | It conducts electricity very well | It is an electrical insulator | It dissolves easily in water | It melts at a very low temperature | Graphene has delocalised electrons, so it conducts very well.
MC | 2 | What are fullerenes? | Molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes | Giant ionic lattices made from carbon | Layers of carbon with no bonds between | Metals that contain carbon atoms | Fullerenes have hollow structures based on rings of carbon atoms.
MC | 2 | How many carbon atoms are in Buckminsterfullerene? | 60 | 6 | 24 | 100 | Buckminsterfullerene is C60, a hollow sphere.
MC | 3 | What is a property of carbon nanotubes? | A very high length to diameter ratio | They are very weak and break easily | They do not conduct electricity | They are soluble in water | Nanotubes are long, thin cylinders that are very strong and conduct.
MC | 3 | What is one use of fullerenes? | Delivering drugs into the body | Making jewellery like diamonds | Lining non-stick pans | Making glass windows | Hollow fullerenes can carry drug molecules. They are also used as catalysts and lubricants.
SA | 2 | What is the formula of Buckminsterfullerene? | C60 | It is a hollow sphere of 60 carbon atoms.
SA | 2 | What shape are carbon nanotubes? | cylinders | Nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes.

lesson: 2.9 - Metallic Bonding
MC | 1 | What is a metal made of? | Positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons | Negative ions surrounded by protons | Small molecules with weak forces | Atoms sharing pairs of electrons | Metals have a giant structure of positive ions with delocalised electrons.
MC | 2 | Why do metals conduct electricity? | Their delocalised electrons can move | Their positive ions can move freely | They contain charged molecules | Their atoms share pairs of electrons | Delocalised electrons carry charge through the metal.
MC | 2 | Why can pure metals be bent and shaped? | Their layers of atoms can slide over each other | Their atoms are held by weak forces | They have no bonds between their atoms | Their electrons are fixed in place | The regular layers of ions slide over each other.
MC | 3 | Why are alloys harder than pure metals? | Different sized atoms distort the layers | The atoms in alloys are all the same size | Alloys have no delocalised electrons | The bonds in alloys are covalent | Different sized atoms make it harder for the layers to slide.
MC | 2 | Why do most metals have high melting points? | Strong metallic bonds must be broken | Their molecules are very large | Their forces are weak but numerous | They contain ionic bonds | The attraction between the ions and delocalised electrons is strong.
MC | 2 | What is an alloy? | A mixture of a metal with other elements | A pure metal with no other atoms | A compound of two non-metals | A metal that has been melted | Alloys such as steel and brass mix a metal with other elements.
SA | 2 | Which alloy is made mainly from iron and carbon? | steel | Steel is an alloy of iron with a little carbon.
SA | 3 | Name the type of electrons that move freely through a metal. | delocalised | Delocalised electrons are not attached to any one atom.

lesson: 2.10 - States of Matter
MC | 1 | Which state of matter has particles that are close together and fixed in place? | Solid | Liquid | Gas | Plasma | In a solid, particles vibrate around fixed positions.
MC | 1 | What is the change from liquid to gas called? | Boiling | Melting | Freezing | Condensing | Boiling (or evaporating) turns a liquid into a gas.
MC | 2 | What happens to the forces between particles when a solid melts? | Some of them are overcome | They all become stronger | Covalent bonds inside break | Nothing changes at all | Energy overcomes some of the forces so particles can move around each other.
MC | 2 | A substance melts at -7 °C and boils at 59 °C. What state is it in at 25 °C? | Liquid | Solid | Gas | Both solid and gas | 25 °C is between the melting and boiling points, so it is a liquid.
MC | 3 | What is one limitation of the simple particle model? | It shows particles as solid, unbonded spheres | It shows particles in a solid as touching | It shows gas particles far apart | It shows particles in a liquid moving | Real particles are not solid spheres and there are forces between them.
MC | 3 | Why does the amount of energy needed to melt a substance depend on its bonding? | Stronger forces need more energy to overcome | Heavier particles always melt more easily | Only ionic substances can melt at all | Melting does not use any energy | The stronger the forces between particles, the higher the melting point.
SA | 1 | What is the change from gas to liquid called? | condensing | Condensation turns a gas back into a liquid.
SA | 1 | What is the change from solid to liquid called? | melting | Melting turns a solid into a liquid at its melting point.

lesson: 2.11 - Nanoparticles
MC | 1 | Roughly how big are nanoparticles? | 1 to 100 nm | 1 to 100 mm | 1 to 100 cm | 0.1 to 1 m | Nanoparticles are between 1 and 100 nanometres across.
MC | 2 | Why do nanoparticles have different properties from the same material in bulk? | They have a very high surface area to volume ratio | They are made of completely different elements | They are always much denser than the bulk material | They have no electrons in their atoms | A large surface area for their volume changes how they react.
MC | 2 | What happens to the surface area to volume ratio when a cube's side is divided by 10? | It increases by 10 times | It decreases by 10 times | It stays the same | It increases by 100 times | Each time the side length falls by 10, the ratio rises by 10.
MC | 2 | Why are nanoparticles useful as catalysts? | Their large surface area speeds up reactions | They take part in the reaction and are used up | They make reactions give out more energy | They stop unwanted reactions happening | More surface area means more places for the reaction to happen.
MC | 3 | Why are some people worried about nanoparticles in sun creams? | Their effects on health are not fully known | They do not block any harmful UV light | They make the cream far too expensive | They change colour in strong sunlight | Nanoparticles may be absorbed into the body and their long-term effects are still being studied.
MC | 2 | How many nanometres are in one metre? | 1 000 000 000 | 1 000 | 1 000 000 | 100 | 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m, so there are a billion in a metre.
SA | 2 | Particles between 100 and 2500 nm across are called fine particles. What letters and number abbreviate this? | PM2.5 | Fine particles are called PM2.5.
SA | 1 | What name is given to particles between 1 and 100 nm across? | nanoparticles | Particles 1 to 100 nm across are nanoparticles.
